A composer starts with a small idea and makes a whole piece from it. The ways of doing that are called compositional devices. There are four main ones: repetition, variation, contrast and transition. Each is carried out with particular techniques, such as sequence, imitation, canon, augmentation and fragmentation. When you analyse a piece, you name the device and the technique and say where it happens.

:::def Compositional device :: a way a composer builds, develops and presents musical ideas: repetition, variation, contrast or transition.
:::def Motif :: a short musical idea, often only a few notes, that a piece is built from.

## Repetition

Repetition is stating an idea again unchanged. It is the simplest device and arguably the most important, because repetition is how a listener learns an idea and recognises it when it returns. A riff, an ostinato and a chorus are all repetition.

:::def Ostinato :: a short pattern repeated over and over, usually in the bass or the rhythm, while other things change above it.

:::clip A2_Bizet_Habanera :: Bizet, Habanera from Carmen :: A repeated bass rhythm under a melody that slides down by semitones.

:::clip A2_Pachelbel_Canon :: Pachelbel, Canon in D :: The same eight bass notes repeat for the whole piece.

## Variation

Variation changes an idea while keeping it recognisable. The techniques below are all forms of variation. Tap each one to see and hear what it does to a four-note motif.

:::def Sequence :: the same idea repeated starting on a higher or lower note, keeping its shape.
:::def Augmentation :: the idea played with longer note values, so it is slower.
:::def Diminution :: the idea played with shorter note values, so it is faster.
:::def Fragmentation :: only part of the idea is used, often repeated or passed around.
:::def Inversion :: the idea turned upside down, so each rise becomes a fall.
:::def Key change (modulation) :: the idea, or the whole music, moves into a new key.

:::example motif-devices

Beethoven's Fifth Symphony begins with a four-note motif and builds the whole first movement from it, by repetition, sequence and fragmentation.

:::clip A2_Beethoven_Sym5_exposition :: Beethoven, Symphony No. 5, exposition :: The motif repeated, moved in sequence and broken into fragments.

## Imitation, canon and countermelody

Imitation is one part copying what another part has just played. A canon is strict imitation: every part plays exactly the same line, each starting a little later, as in a round. A countermelody is a second melody, different from the first, played at the same time with it.

:::def Imitation :: one voice or instrument copies a phrase that another has just played.
:::def Canon :: strict imitation in which every part has the same line, entering one after another.
:::def Countermelody :: a second, independent melody played at the same time as the main one.

:::example canon

:::clip A2_Monteverdi_PurTiMiro :: Monteverdi, Pur ti miro :: Two voices imitating each other over a repeated bass.

## Contrast

Contrast is introducing something clearly new: a different melody, key, texture, dynamic level or tempo. A chorus after a verse, a slow middle section in a fast piece and a new tune in a new key are all contrast. Without contrast, repetition becomes dull; without repetition, contrast has nothing to contrast with.

## Transition

A transition is the music that moves from one idea or section to the next, so that the change is prepared rather than sudden. It can be a few bars that build up to a new section, a link that changes key, or a passage that winds down. Techniques include a crescendo, a change of harmony, a rising sequence that leads to a new key, or a bridge in a song.

:::def Transition :: music that connects one idea or section to another, preparing the change.
:::def Bridge :: a contrasting section in a song that leads back to the chorus or into the final section.

## Naming devices in a response

In a listening response, say what the idea is, what happens to it, and where. "The four-note motif (0:00) is repeated a step lower in sequence (0:06), then fragmented into two-note pairs passed between the strings (0:22)" names the device, the technique and the time. Use the term, then the evidence.

:::aside Quick check :: If you can hum the idea back after one hearing, repetition is doing its job. If you can tell it is the same idea but something about it has changed, that is variation. If you cannot connect it to anything before, that is contrast.

:::quiz
