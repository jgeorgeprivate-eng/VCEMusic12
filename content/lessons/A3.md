Texture is the number of layers in the music and how they relate to one another. It is one of the elements of music, and one of the easiest to hear once you know the names. The lesson gives you four textures and one process, layering, and lets you build each one yourself.

:::def Texture :: the number of layers of sound in the music and the relationship between them.

## Monophonic

One melody and nothing else. It does not matter how many people play or sing it: if everyone has the same line, the texture is monophonic. Plainchant, a solo oboe, and a crowd singing the national anthem without accompaniment are all monophonic.

:::def Monophonic :: a single melodic line with no accompaniment and no other parts, whether sung by one person or many.

:::clip A3_Animuccia_Introit :: Introit, Resurrexi et adhuc tecum sum :: Unaccompanied chant: one line, many voices.

## Homophonic, and melody with accompaniment

Homophonic texture has one main melody with other parts supporting it. The strictest kind is chordal homophony, where all the parts move together in the same rhythm, as in a hymn or a choir singing in block chords. The looser and more common kind is melody and accompaniment: a tune on top with chords, a bass line or a pattern underneath. Most songs, most piano music and most pop music are melody and accompaniment.

:::def Homophonic :: one melody that stands out, with the other parts supporting it rather than competing with it.
:::def Melody and accompaniment :: a homophonic texture in which the melody is clearly on top and the other parts form a background of chords, bass or patterns.

:::clip A3_Byrd_Civitas :: Byrd, Civitas sancti tui :: Passages where the voices move together in chords, and passages where they enter one by one.

:::clip A3_Bach_Air :: Bach, Air on the G String :: A long melody over a walking bass.

## Polyphonic

Polyphonic texture has two or more independent melodies at the same time. No single line is the tune and the rest accompaniment; each line could stand on its own. Canons, rounds and fugues are polyphonic. So is a duet in which the two voices weave around each other.

:::def Polyphonic :: two or more independent melodies sounding together, each with its own shape and rhythm.
:::def Fugue :: a polyphonic piece in which one short theme, the subject, is introduced by each voice in turn and then developed.

:::clip A3_Bach_FugueCminor :: Bach, Fugue in C minor :: Three voices enter one after another with the same subject.

:::clip A3_Delibes_FlowerDuet :: Delibes, Flower Duet :: Two voices together in thirds, then weaving independently.

## Layering

Layering is the process of adding parts one at a time, so the texture thickens, or taking them away, so it thins. It is a device for building tension, marking sections and making a return feel like an arrival. Much electronic and popular music is built this way: drums, then bass, then chords, then the voice.

:::def Layering :: adding or removing parts one by one so the texture grows thicker or thinner.

:::clip A3_Pachelbel_Canon :: Pachelbel, Canon in D :: The bass alone, then one part, then two, then three.

## Describing texture

Say which texture it is, how thick it is (how many parts), and whether it changes. "Melody and accompaniment throughout, three layers: solo oboe, sustained string chords and a plucked bass; at 1:10 the strings drop out, leaving the oboe alone for two bars" is a complete description. Watch for changes of texture, because they almost always mark something important.

:::aside Quick check :: Ask two questions of any passage. How many different things are going on at once? And is one of them clearly the tune? One thing: monophonic. Several things with a clear tune: homophonic. Several things, each a tune: polyphonic.

:::quiz
