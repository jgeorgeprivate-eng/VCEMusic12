Every piece of music is organised somehow. It has sections, and the sections repeat, vary and contrast in a plan. This plan is the structure or form of the piece. A small number of plans account for a very large amount of music, and once you know them you can hear the shape of a piece on first listening. Sections are usually labelled with letters: A for the first idea, B for a different one, C for a third.

:::def Structure (form) :: the plan of sections in a piece and the order they come in.
:::def Section :: a part of a piece with its own material, usually several phrases long, that can be given a letter name.

## Binary: A B

Two sections, each usually repeated: A A B B. The first section often ends away from home, on the dominant, and the second brings the music back to the tonic. Dances from the baroque period, such as minuets, are usually binary.

:::example form-player form=binary caption="Binary form: two sections. Tap play and watch the blocks."

:::clip A4_Bach_MinuetInG :: Minuet in G from the Notebook for Anna Magdalena Bach :: Two sections, each repeated.

## Ternary: A B A

Three sections where the third is the first again: a statement, a contrast, and a return. The B section often changes key, tempo or mood. Many songs, slow movements and character pieces are ternary.

:::example form-player form=ternary caption="Ternary form: the opening returns after a contrasting middle."

:::clip A4_Chopin_Raindrop :: Chopin, Raindrop Prelude :: A calm major A section, a stormy minor B section, and A returns.

## Rondo: A B A C A

A main section keeps coming back between different episodes. The returning section is the refrain. A rondo can be longer, A B A C A B A, but the idea is the same: home, away, home, away, home.

:::example form-player form=rondo caption="Rondo form: A returns after every new section."

:::clip A4_Beethoven_FurElise :: Beethoven, Für Elise :: The famous opening is the A section of a rondo.

## Theme and variations

A theme, usually a short tune in two halves, is stated and then repeated several times, each time changed in some way: a new rhythm, faster notes, a minor key, a different register, a new accompaniment. The length and chords usually stay the same, so you can always hear the theme underneath.

:::example form-player form=variations caption="A theme and two variations: the same tune in faster notes, then in the minor."

:::clip A4_Paganini_Caprice24 :: Paganini, Caprice No. 24 :: A theme and a run of variations for solo violin.

## Bar form: A A B

A phrase is heard twice, then a different phrase follows. It is an old song form, used in medieval and renaissance songs, hymns and many later melodies.

:::example form-player form=bar caption="Bar form: A twice, then B."

:::clip A4_SaintSaens_Aquarium :: Saint-Saëns, Aquarium :: The opening phrase twice, then a new longer phrase.

## Verse and chorus

The plan of most songs. Verses have the same music with different words each time and tell the story. The chorus has the same music and the same words every time and carries the main idea. Many songs add an introduction, a bridge (a contrasting section late in the song) and a coda or outro.

:::def Verse :: a section of a song whose music repeats with new words each time.
:::def Chorus :: the section of a song that returns with the same words and music, usually the hook.

:::example form-player form=verse caption="Verse, chorus, verse, chorus."

:::clip A4_DrunkenSailor :: Drunken Sailor (traditional) :: Verses with new words, and the same chorus each time.

:::clip A4_MidnightOil_DeadHeart :: Midnight Oil, The Dead Heart :: A riff-based song in verse and chorus form.

## Riff-based

A riff is a short, catchy pattern, usually on guitar, bass or keyboard, repeated over and over as the foundation of a song or section. Much rock, funk and blues is riff-based: the riff is the identity of the piece, and verses and choruses sit on top of it.

:::def Riff :: a short repeated pattern that a piece or section is built on.

:::example riff

:::clip A4_EmmaDonovan_DeadHeart :: Emma Donovan, The Dead Heart :: The same riff-based song in another version.

## 12-bar blues

A pattern of twelve bars using the three primary chords, I, IV and V, in a set order. The same twelve bars repeat for the whole piece, each time round called a chorus. Players vary the details, for example a IV chord in bar 2 or a V chord in bar 12, but the twelve-bar shape stays. Blues, early rock and roll and much jazz use it.

:::example blues

:::clip A4_MilesDavis_AllBlues :: Miles Davis, All Blues :: A 12-bar blues in 6/8.

## Head, solos, head

The jazz plan. The band plays the tune, called the head, then each player in turn improvises a solo over the tune's chord pattern, and then the head returns to finish. Listen for the moment the written tune gives way to improvisation.

:::def Head :: the written tune of a jazz piece, played at the start and the end.
:::def Improvisation :: music made up on the spot, usually over a set pattern of chords.

:::example head-solos-head

:::clip A4_NightInTunisia :: Gillespie and Parker, A Night in Tunisia :: Head, solos, head.

## Working out the form of a piece

Listen once all the way through. Mark the time whenever something clearly new starts or something returns. Give each section a letter, using the same letter for a return. Then match the pattern of letters to the plans above. If none fits exactly, describe the pattern you found in letters and say which plan it is closest to.

:::aside Quick check :: Returning material is the key. If the opening comes back once after a contrast, think ternary. If it keeps coming back between new sections, think rondo. If it comes back changed each time, think variations. If it is a short pattern that never stops, think riff.

:::quiz
