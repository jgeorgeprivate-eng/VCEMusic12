A piece of music is not fixed by its notes. Every performance is a set of choices about tempo, dynamics, articulation, tone colour and balance, and every arrangement changes the instruments, the texture and sometimes the structure. Comparing two versions of the same work is the clearest way to see how those choices create effect, because the notes stay the same and only the choices change.

:::def Interpretation :: the choices a performer makes about how to play the written notes: tempo, dynamics, articulation, phrasing, tone.
:::def Arrangement :: a version of a work rewritten for different instruments or voices, or in a different style.
:::def Cover version :: a new recording of a song by a different performer, often in a different style.

## Adding a melody to an accompaniment

Bach's Prelude in C major is a sequence of broken chords with no tune on top. Gounod wrote a melody to go over it, and the result, the Bach-Gounod Ave Maria, has a completely different effect even though every note of the Bach is still there.

:::clip A7_Bach_Prelude :: Bach, Prelude in C major :: Broken chords with no melody.

:::clip A7_BachGounod_AveMaria :: Bach and Gounod, Ave Maria :: The same prelude with a sung melody added.

Listen for what changes: the texture goes from a single line of broken chords to melody and accompaniment; the attention moves from the harmony to the voice; the tempo usually slows; and the piece gains words and a clear climax.

## A song arranged for piano alone

Schubert's Erlkönig is a song for voice and piano in which the singer plays four characters and the piano gallops throughout. Liszt arranged it for piano alone. The arrangement must do with one instrument what the song does with a voice and a piano.

:::clip A7_Schubert_Erlkonig_song :: Schubert, Erlkönig :: Voice and piano.

:::clip A7_Schubert_Erlkonig_Liszt :: Schubert, Erlkönig, arranged by Liszt :: Piano alone.

Listen for how Liszt shows the characters without words: register (the boy high, the father low), dynamics and articulation. Then ask what is lost and what is gained. The words and the human voice are lost; the gallop and the drama can be even more violent on the piano alone.

## A cover in another style

Tito Puente wrote Oye Como Va as a cha-cha-chá for a Latin dance band. Santana recorded it as a rock song. The melody and the chords are the same; the instruments, the rhythm section and the tempo are not.

:::clip A7_TitoPuente_OyeComoVa :: Tito Puente, Oye Como Va :: The original.

:::clip A7_Santana_OyeComoVa :: Santana, Oye Como Va :: The rock version.

## Your own recording against a professional one

Record one of your pieces in the practice room and listen to it beside a professional recording of the same piece. Use the template: tempo, dynamics, articulation, tone colour, phrasing. The point is not to find fault but to hear the choices the professional made and decide which you want to make yourself. Your recordings stay on your phone.

## How to compare two versions

Use a table with the elements down the side and the two versions across the top. Fill in a line for each element, then write one paragraph on the biggest differences and their effect. End with a sentence on which version does what better, with a reason. Keep the comparison to the music, not the performers' fame.

:::aside Quick check :: The strongest comparisons name a difference, say which element it belongs to, and say what effect it has. "The Santana version is faster, with electric guitar and organ instead of brass and flute (tempo, tone colour), which makes it driving rather than danceable."

:::quiz
