Music is made by people in a time and a place, for a reason. Knowing who made a piece, when, where and for whom is its context. Context explains choices that would otherwise seem arbitrary: why a waltz is in 3/4, why a rag has a steady left hand, why a symphony opens so loudly. It is the last part of the listening template, and the part that turns a description into an understanding.

:::def Context :: the circumstances in which a piece was made and performed: its time, place, purpose, audience and the people involved.

## Three kinds of context

Historical context is the period: what instruments existed, what styles were current, what had just been invented. Social context is the use: music for dancing, for church, for a concert hall, for a film, for a parade, for a phone. Cultural context is the tradition: the people and practices a piece belongs to, and what it borrows from other traditions.

:::def Historical context :: the period a work comes from and what was possible and usual then.
:::def Social context :: what the music was for and who it was performed to.
:::def Cultural context :: the tradition the music belongs to and the traditions it draws on.

## Ragtime and jazz

Scott Joplin published Maple Leaf Rag in 1899 in Missouri. Ragtime was piano music sold as sheet music for people to play at home and in bars, with a steady left-hand beat and a syncopated right hand. Those syncopations, and the blend of African American rhythm with European harmony, were a root of jazz. Twenty-five years later, George Gershwin wrote Rhapsody in Blue for a concert in New York, taking jazz rhythms and blue notes into a piece for piano and orchestra. The two works show a tradition starting in one place and reaching another.

## The waltz in Vienna

Johann Strauss II wrote The Blue Danube in 1867 for Vienna, where the waltz was the fashionable ballroom dance. The music exists for dancing couples turning once a bar: the 3/4 metre, the oom-pah-pah accompaniment and the chain of tunes each a little more exciting than the last all come from that purpose. The waltz had once been thought improper because the partners held each other; by Strauss's time it was the sound of a whole city.

:::clip A8_Strauss_BlueDanube :: Johann Strauss II, The Blue Danube :: A waltz for the ballrooms of Vienna.

## Beethoven, at home and in the hall

Für Elise is a short piano piece Beethoven wrote around 1810, found and published only in 1867. It is music for an amateur to play at home on a piano, and everything about it is sized for that: a single player, a small range of dynamics, a tune that is easy to remember. The Fifth Symphony, first performed in Vienna in 1808, is the opposite case. It was written for a large orchestra in a public concert for a paying audience, and its opening is built to seize a hall. The same composer wrote both, for different places and purposes.

:::clip A8_Beethoven_FurElise :: Beethoven, Für Elise :: Music for the home.

:::clip A8_Beethoven_Sym5 :: Beethoven, Symphony No. 5 :: Music for the concert hall.

## A private joke

Saint-Saëns wrote The Carnival of the Animals in 1886 for a private concert among friends, as a joke, and refused to let it be published in his lifetime because he feared it would damage his reputation as a serious composer. Aquarium, the gentlest movement, comes from a piece its composer did not want the public to hear.

:::clip A8_SaintSaens_Carnival :: Saint-Saëns, Aquarium :: From a work written for friends.

## Using context in a response

Context should explain the music, not replace it. A sentence of context earns its place when it accounts for something you can hear: "Written for dancing, so the 3/4 metre and the oom-pah-pah accompaniment never change." Facts that do not connect to a feature of the music, such as a composer's birthday, add nothing. For your own works in My works, find three facts: when and where the piece was written, what it was for, and one thing about the composer or performer that shaped it.
