Once you have one or two phrases, you can organise them into a short piece by choosing a plan from lesson A4: binary, ternary, rondo, bar form or verse and chorus. The plan decides what repeats, what contrasts and what returns, and it is the clearest thing you can point to when you explain how your piece is organised.

:::def Plan (form) :: the order of sections in a piece, written in letters such as A B A.

## Choosing a plan

Each plan suits a different amount of material. With one phrase, write a second contrasting phrase and use binary (A B) or bar form (A A B). With two phrases, use ternary (A B A) for a satisfying return. With three, use rondo (A B A C A). Verse and chorus suits a song: the verse carries new words each time and the chorus is the catchy part.

:::example form-player form=rondo caption="A B A C A: needs three ideas, and brings the first back twice."

## Making sections contrast

The B section should differ from A in something you can name: a new melody, a different register, the minor key, a different rhythm, a thinner texture, a softer dynamic. One or two differences are enough. The return of A then sounds like coming home, and you can make it feel final by ending it on the tonic with a longer note.

## Transitions

A short link between sections stops the change from sounding abrupt: a bar of the bass alone, a rising scale into the new section, or a held note. In a four-bar exercise a transition can be as little as one beat.

## Structure planner

Save two or three short pieces in the melody writer, then come back here. Choose a plan, give each section one of your saved pieces, and play the whole plan through. The sections light up as they play.

:::tool structure-planner

## In your folio

Write the plan in letters, say which piece or phrase is each section, and name the contrast between them: "Ternary, A B A. A is the four-bar phrase in G major; B is the four-bar phrase in E minor with a walking bass, softer; A returns unchanged."

:::aside Quick check :: Play your plan and count the sections. If the listener cannot tell where one section ends and the next begins, the sections need more contrast or a clearer ending.

:::quiz
