Up to now the lessons have dealt with notes one after another. Harmony is what happens when notes sound together. The simplest and most important chord is the triad, a chord of three notes. Almost every piece you play or hear is built on triads.

:::def Harmony :: two or more notes sounding at the same time, and the way chords follow one another.
:::def Chord :: three or more notes sounding together.
:::def Triad :: a chord of three notes built in thirds: a root, a third above it and a fifth above it.

## Building a triad

Take any note of a scale and call it the root. Add the note a third above (skip one letter) and the note a third above that (skip another letter). Those three notes are a triad. On the staff a triad in root position stacks neatly: line, line, line or space, space, space.

:::def Root :: the note a triad is built on and named after.
:::def Third and fifth :: the other two notes of a triad, a third and a fifth above the root.

:::example triad key=C degree=1 caption="The triad on C: C E G. Tap to hear it as a chord, then note by note."

## Triads on the scale degrees

You can build a triad on every degree of the major scale, using only the notes of the scale. Each one is named with a Roman numeral for its degree. This course uses five of them: I, ii, IV, V and vi. Capital numerals are used for major triads and small numerals for minor triads.

:::def Roman numeral :: the symbol for a chord by its scale degree, I for the chord on degree 1, V for the chord on degree 5, and so on.

:::example triads key=C

Tap each chord to hear it. I, IV and V sound bright and are major triads. ii and vi sound darker and are minor triads. The chord on I is also called the tonic chord, and the chord on V is called the dominant chord.

:::def Tonic chord :: the triad on degree 1, written I. The home chord.
:::def Dominant chord :: the triad on degree 5, written V. It pulls strongly back to the tonic chord.

## Major and minor triads

The difference between a major and a minor triad is the third. In a major triad the third is a major 3rd above the root, four semitones. In a minor triad it is a minor 3rd above the root, three semitones. The fifth is a perfect 5th in both. Compare C major, C E G, with the minor triad on the same root, C E♭ G.

:::example melody notes="C4h E4h G4h | C4h Eb4h G4h" key=C time=3/4 caption="C major triad, then C minor triad. Only the middle note changes." endbar=yes

:::example triad key=C degree=2 caption="ii in C major: D minor, D F A. The third, D to F, is a minor 3rd."

:::example triad key=C degree=5 caption="V in C major: G major, G B D. The third, G to B, is a major 3rd."

## Triads in other keys

The same five chords exist in every major key, built from that key's scale. In G major, I is G major, ii is A minor, IV is C major, V is D major and vi is E minor. The Roman numerals stay the same; only the letter names change with the key.

## Hearing triads

When you hear a triad, listen first for major or minor. Then, if you know the key, listen for where it sits. The tonic chord sounds settled. The dominant sounds as if it wants to move. IV is open and a little unsettled. The minor chords, ii and vi, sound softer and more inward. The scale and chord builder plays each triad as a block chord and broken into its notes.
