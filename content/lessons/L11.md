Chords rarely stand alone. They follow one another in patterns that give music its sense of movement and arrival. A sequence of chords is a chord progression. The same few progressions appear in thousands of pieces, so learning to hear them tells you a great deal about any music you meet.

:::def Chord progression :: a sequence of chords, written as Roman numerals so the same pattern can be named in any key.

## V to I

The most important move in harmony is from the dominant chord, V, to the tonic chord, I. V contains the leading note, degree 7, which pulls up to the tonic, and its root, degree 5, which falls or rises to the tonic. Together they make V to I sound like an arrival. This move ends many phrases and most pieces in a major key. A chord progression that ends a phrase is called a cadence.

:::def Cadence :: the chords that end a phrase. V to I is the strongest cadence, called a perfect cadence.

:::example progression key=C id=V-I caption="V to I in C major: G major to C major. Tap to hear the arrival."

## ii to V to I

Adding ii before V makes a longer approach to the tonic. ii is a minor chord, and it leads smoothly into V because its root, degree 2, is a fifth above the root of V. This progression is everywhere in jazz and in many songs.

:::example progression key=C id=ii-V-I caption="ii – V – I in C major: D minor, G major, C major."

## I to IV to V to I

This is the backbone of folk, blues, rock and classical music alike. It starts at home, moves away to IV, builds tension on V and returns home. The three chords I, IV and V are called the primary chords, and between them they contain every note of the major scale, so any melody in the key can be harmonised with them.

:::def Primary chords :: the three major triads of a key, I, IV and V.

:::example progression key=C id=I-IV-V-I caption="I – IV – V – I in C major: C, F, G, C."

## I to V to vi to IV

This four-chord loop is one of the most common progressions in pop music of the last fifty years. Instead of returning home after V, it moves to vi, the relative minor, which gives a bittersweet turn, and then to IV before the loop repeats. Because it does not end on I it can go round and round.

:::example progression key=C id=I-V-vi-IV caption="I – V – vi – IV in C major: C, G, A minor, F."

## The same progressions in other keys

A progression keeps its Roman numerals in every key; only the letter names change. In G major, I – IV – V – I is G, C, D, G. In F major it is F, B♭, C, F. When you identify a progression by ear, you are hearing the relationship between the chords, not their letter names, so the key does not matter.

:::example progression key=G id=I-IV-V-I caption="I – IV – V – I in G major."

:::example progression key=F id=I-V-vi-IV caption="I – V – vi – IV in F major: F, C, D minor, B♭."

## Hearing a progression

To tell apart the four progressions in these lessons, listen for three things. Does the final chord sound like home? If so it ends on I. Is there a minor chord, and where does it come? ii comes just before V; vi comes after V. How many chords are there? Two means V – I. Three means ii – V – I. Four ending at home means I – IV – V – I. Four not ending at home means I – V – vi – IV.
