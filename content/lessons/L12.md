Staff notation is the most complete way to write music down, but it is not the only one. Different kinds of music and different tasks use different methods. In this course you need to recognise five of them, read them, and choose the right one for a job.

:::def Documentation :: any way of recording music on paper or screen so that it can be read, studied or performed again.

## Staff notation

Staff notation, covered in the earlier lessons, shows exact pitch and rhythm, plus dynamics, articulation and tempo. It suits music where every note is fixed, such as classical and ensemble music. Its weakness is that it takes training to read and does not show how a sound is produced, for example on a guitar or in a studio.

## Graphic notation

Graphic notation uses shapes, lines and symbols instead of notes. Height on the page usually stands for pitch, length from left to right for time, and size or darkness for loudness. It is used for music with sounds that staff notation cannot show well: electronic sounds, noise, glides between pitches, or improvised passages. Each piece explains its own symbols, so a graphic score always comes with a key.

:::def Graphic notation :: notation that uses drawn shapes and lines rather than standard notes to show roughly what should happen and when.

:::example graphic-score

## Tablature

Tablature, or TAB, is written for fretted string instruments such as guitar, bass and ukulele. Each horizontal line is a string, with the thinnest, highest string at the top. A number on a line is the fret to press on that string, and 0 means an open string. TAB shows exactly where to put your fingers, which staff notation does not, but on its own it shows rhythm poorly. It is usually printed under a staff.

:::def Tablature (TAB) :: notation for fretted instruments in which lines are strings and numbers are frets.

:::example tab

## Lead sheets and chord symbols

A lead sheet gives the melody in staff notation with chord symbols written above it, and often the lyrics below. A chord symbol names a chord by its root letter and its type: C means C major, Dm means D minor, G7 means G with an added seventh. The performer plays or improvises an accompaniment from the symbols. Lead sheets are the standard way to write songs, jazz and musical theatre, and they are the format of most fake books.

:::def Lead sheet :: a melody with chord symbols above it. The accompaniment is left to the performer.
:::def Chord symbol :: a short code for a chord, such as C, Dm, F or G7, written above the staff.

:::example lead-sheet

## Rhythm grids

A rhythm grid divides a bar into equal boxes and marks the boxes where notes begin. It shows rhythm clearly without any staff notation, which makes it good for drum patterns, percussion parts and for composing rhythms in a sequencer, where it is called a step sequencer or piano roll. It cannot show pitch unless each row is given a note, which is what a piano roll does.

## Annotated track listings

An annotated track listing describes a recording section by section against a timeline. It records the time each section starts, a name for it (introduction, verse, chorus, bridge, solo, coda) and notes on what the music does there: which instruments play, the dynamics, the texture, a key change. It documents music that exists only as a recording, such as a song you are analysing, or a piece you have made in a studio. It is also the standard way to write a listening analysis.

:::def Annotated track listing :: a timed list of the sections of a recording with notes on the musical features of each.

:::example track-listing

## Choosing a method

Match the method to the music and the purpose. To give an ensemble every note, use staff notation. To show a guitarist where the fingers go, use TAB. To write a song so a band can play it, use a lead sheet. To program a drum pattern or write a rhythm quickly, use a rhythm grid. To describe a recording or an electronic piece, use an annotated track listing, and for sounds that staff notation cannot capture, use graphic notation. Often two are combined, such as staff notation with TAB under it, or a lead sheet with a rhythm grid for the drums.

:::aside Quick check :: When a question asks you to document a musical idea, say which method you chose and why it suits that idea. The reason matters as much as the choice.

:::quiz
