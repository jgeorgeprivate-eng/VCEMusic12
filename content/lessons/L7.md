An interval is the distance between two notes. Naming intervals lets you describe melodies and chords precisely, and recognising them by ear is one of the most useful listening skills there is. Every interval has a number and a quality.

:::def Interval :: the distance in pitch between two notes, either played one after the other (melodic) or at the same time (harmonic).

## The number

The number of an interval is found by counting letter names from the lower note to the higher one, counting both ends. C up to E is a third, because C D E is three letters. C up to G is a fifth. C up to the next C is an eighth, which is called an octave. Two notes of the same pitch are a unison.

:::def Unison :: two notes of the same pitch. It counts as the interval number one.
:::def Octave :: the interval from a note to the next note of the same name, eight letter names up.

Sharps and flats never change the number. C to E♭ is still a third, because it still covers the letters C D E.

## The quality

Two intervals can have the same number but a different size in semitones. C to E is four semitones; E to G is three semitones. Both are thirds, but they sound different. The quality names tell them apart. In the major scale, the intervals from the tonic are: major 2nd, major 3rd, perfect 4th, perfect 5th, major 6th, major 7th and perfect octave.

:::def Major interval :: the size of the 2nd, 3rd, 6th or 7th found between the tonic and that degree of a major scale.
:::def Minor interval :: one semitone smaller than the major interval of the same number.
:::def Perfect interval :: the name given to unisons, 4ths, 5ths and octaves, which do not come in major and minor sizes.

Tap each interval to see and hear it, first as two notes one after the other and then played together.

:::example intervals key=C

The semitone counts are worth learning: a major 2nd is two semitones, a major 3rd four, a perfect 4th five, a perfect 5th seven, a major 6th nine, a major 7th eleven and an octave twelve.

## Intervals between other notes of the scale

Not every interval in a major scale starts on the tonic. Between other degrees you find the minor intervals. E to G is a minor 3rd, three semitones. D to F is also a minor 3rd. B to D is a minor 3rd too. The minor 2nd, one semitone, appears between E and F and between B and C. The minor 6th and minor 7th appear between other pairs. All the intervals in this course are diatonic, which means both notes belong to the major scale being used.

:::def Diatonic interval :: an interval in which both notes belong to the scale of the key. The course uses diatonic intervals in major keys with up to one sharp or flat.

:::example melody notes="E4q G4q E4h | D4q F4q D4h | B3q D4q B3h | E4q F4q E4h" key=C time=4/4 caption="Minor 3rds between E and G, D and F, and B and D, then a minor 2nd between E and F." endbar=yes

## Working out an interval

Take the two notes. Count the letter names from the lower to the higher to get the number. Then count the semitones. Compare the count with the major scale table above: if it matches the major scale size, the interval is major or perfect; if it is one semitone smaller than a major interval, it is minor.
