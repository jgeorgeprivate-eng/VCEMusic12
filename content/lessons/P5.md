Playing in a group is a different skill from playing alone. The notes are usually easier; the listening is harder. In an ensemble you are one layer of a texture, and your job changes bar by bar: sometimes you have the melody, sometimes an accompaniment, sometimes a rest you must count exactly. Both units ask for at least one group performance, so these habits matter.

:::def Ensemble :: a group of players or singers performing together, from a duet to an orchestra.
:::def Balance :: the relative loudness of the parts, so that the melody is heard and the accompaniment supports it.

## Listening

Know who has the tune at every moment and listen for them. If it is you, project; if it is not, play under them. Listen to the bass for the beat and the harmony. Listen to the player next to you for tuning. When two of you have the same note, one of you adjusts; agree in rehearsal who.

## Balance

A part marked forte in an accompaniment is softer than a part marked forte in the melody. Dynamics in a group are relative. The rule is simple: if you cannot hear the melody over yourself, you are too loud. Oboe carries, so this applies to you more than to most.

## Tuning

Tune to the same reference before you begin; the app's tuner and drone help. Then keep tuning while you play. Long notes and unisons are where tuning shows. Sustained notes with the drone, and the play-back exercise in the practice room, train the ear to hear beats between two notes that are almost in tune and to remove them.

## Counting and cues

Count rests in bars, and mark the cues in your part: the instrument that plays just before you come in, and its figure. Watch the leader or conductor at starts, stops, changes of tempo and the final note. A cue is given a beat before it is needed, so look up a beat early.

## Rehearsal habits

Arrive with the notes learned; rehearsal is for putting the parts together, not for learning them. Mark your part in pencil: breaths, cues, dynamics decided in rehearsal. Make eye contact at entries. When something goes wrong, keep going; the group is the priority. Afterwards, note in the journal what the group needs to fix and what you need to fix.

## Metronome and drone

The metronome in the practice room has 4/4, 3/4, 2/4 and 6/8 with subdivisions, and a tap tempo. Use it for the passages where the group loses time, and practise entries after long rests by counting them aloud against it.

:::aside Quick check :: Before a rehearsal, be able to answer three questions about your part: where do I have the tune, where do I accompany, and where are my hardest entries?

:::quiz
